# Escalation Memo - <one-line subject>

> **Why this document exists.** Something has gone wrong. The memo exists so
> that the person who can fix it gets, in under two minutes of reading, the four
> things they need: what happened, what it costs, what you have already done,
> and what you need from them. Escalating badly is worse than escalating late,
> because a vague escalation converts one stuck person into two.
>
> **Who reads it.** Your delivery lead and, often, the Northwind sponsor. Assume
> it gets forwarded without your commentary attached. Write it so it survives
> being read by someone who has not thought about this engagement all week.
>
> **Time budget: 45 minutes, Day 5.** Something will have gone wrong by Day 5.
> If you think nothing has, you have not looked at the data closely enough or
> you are protecting somebody, including possibly yourself.
>

### What a weak version looks like

A weak escalation buries the ask in the last paragraph, mixes facts with
interpretation so the reader cannot tell which is which, presents no options, or
presents options with no recommendation. The worst version is written to assign
blame, which guarantees the next person in the chain spends their time on the
politics rather than on the problem. Two-minute test: hand it to someone with no
context and ask what you want from them. If they cannot say, rewrite it.
